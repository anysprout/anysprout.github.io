ECO 631: Applied Policy Methods

Spring 2025

Coursework

This project re-calculates and stress-tests a government cost–benefit analysis using the authors’ own stated assumptions. It was selected because it highlights independent verification, present value modeling, and the identification of potential overstatements in reported benefit figures.

Portfolio Assignment: View Report (PDF)  |  View Calculations (PDF)

Learning Objectives

Identify the costs, benefits and tradeoffs involved in economic policy making

  • Derive research questions concerning policy from conceptual and theoretical foundations.
  • Integrate institutional context into assessment of policy.
  • Identify private and social impacts of policy.

Compare and contrast alternative economic policy assessments to reach a defensible recommendation

  • Characterize differences in methodology and data used to assess policy.
  • Assess weaknesses and strengths of alternative approaches to policy assessment.
  • Propose and support an assessment of a policy-related body of research.

Present the motivation, methods and conclusions from applied economic research

  • Describe and motivate an applied economic research question.
  • Identify and explain why particular data and methods are appropriate for a given empirical analysis.
  • Interpret and communicate results of an empirical analysis.

Reflection

ECO 631 helped me learn how to work through a wide range of technical writing without getting lost in the details. A major skill I developed in this course was learning how to read reports outside my immediate area of expertise, identify the assumptions holding the analysis together, and evaluate whether the conclusions were actually supported by the evidence. That gave me more confidence in engaging with complex material across disciplines without feeling like I needed to become a specialist in every field before I could assess the strength of an argument.

The course also helped me understand critique as a constructive analytical skill rather than just disagreement. By evaluating research design, assumptions, and claims, I became better at seeing what makes an argument credible and what makes it vulnerable. Working with an official government report was especially useful because it showed that formal authority does not guarantee complete or flawless analysis. It also gave me more experience thinking about uncertainty, including how results can change under different assumptions and conditions. That process helped me become more careful in reading evidence and more deliberate in how I support my own claims.
